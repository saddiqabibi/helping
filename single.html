How to Help a Struggling Neighbour With Dignity

HomeStories & UpdatesThis Guide

By the Poor Foundation team  |  October 2026  |  About a 5-minute read

Most people who need help will not ask for it. They are worried about what neighbours will think, or about being a burden. That is why the way we offer help matters almost as much as what we offer. This short guide collects practical habits for helping someone nearby without making them feel small.

1. Notice quietly, and do not assume

Signs that a family may be struggling are rarely dramatic. A child who suddenly stops coming to school, a shop owner who is cutting back on lunch, or a neighbour who stops going to the doctor can all be clues. They are only clues, though. Before offering anything, spend a little time talking and listening. You may find the real problem is different from the one you imagined.

2. Offer in private

An offer made in front of other people can feel like a public announcement. Speak to the person alone, or send a trusted relative. Keep the conversation short and warm, and let them know there is no obligation. A line such as "I was going to the market anyway, may I bring some flour for you?" is gentler than "I heard you are having a hard time."

3. Ask what would help most

People know their own situation better than anyone. One family may need groceries, another may need a school fee paid before a deadline, and a third may need a lift to a clinic. If you are unsure, ask. Giving what is useful is better than giving what is convenient for you.

4. Think about what you give

  • Food and household basics such as flour, rice, lentils and cooking oil are always useful. Check expiry dates, and avoid giving items you would not use yourself.
  • School costs work best when paid straight to the school, so the money is used for the child.
  • Medicine should only be given on a doctor's prescription. Please do not hand over leftover or expired tablets.
  • Cash is sometimes what a family needs most, but it is also the hardest to give well. Consider whether another form of help would solve the problem more directly.

If you give zakat or sadaqah, make sure you understand who is eligible to receive it. When in doubt, ask a trusted scholar.

5. Protect their privacy

Do not photograph the family, and do not post about the help you gave. A picture that raises your standing in the community can cost them theirs. If you want to inspire others, talk about the idea in general terms and leave out names, faces and street names.

6. Keep it going, if you can

A single parcel solves a week. Hardship often lasts longer, so a follow-up call a few weeks later can matter more than the first visit. Ask how things are going and whether the need has changed. If you cannot keep helping, say so honestly and look for others who can.

7. Know your limits and involve others

Some problems are bigger than one person can handle, such as serious illness, a lost home or a family in danger. In those cases, bring in a clinic, a school, a local organisation or the authorities, depending on the situation. Asking for help for someone else is part of helping.

A short checklist

  • Have I spoken to them privately?
  • Have I asked what they actually need?
  • Is what I am giving safe, useful and in good condition?
  • Have I avoided photos, names and public mention?
  • Do I have a plan to check in later?

If you would like to help families in Lahore but do not know where to begin, you are welcome to volunteer with us, support a project, or ask us a question.
